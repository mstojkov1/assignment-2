The data

We used ProPublica’s database of civilian complaints against NYPD officers, built from records of the city’s Civilian Complaint Review Board (CCRB). Each row is one allegation against one officer, not one complaint, and a single complaint can contain several allegations. Our file has … allegations from … complaints involving … officers.

Read this before the charts. Our copy is a subset of the full database. It appears to be the first rows of the file when it is grouped by each officer’s current command, so it is dominated by a handful of mostly Brooklyn precincts and detective squads. It is not a random sample. Every percentage on this page describes this subset only and should not be read as an NYPD-wide figure. One final row of our copy was cut off and was dropped.

Write-up: white hat

Dataset. ProPublica’s NYPD civilian complaints database, from CCRB records. Each row is an allegation (… in our subset), classified into four CCRB types (Force, Abuse of Authority, Discourtesy, Offensive Language) and ending in a board finding. Because our file is a non-random subset concentrated in a few Brooklyn commands, we say so above the charts and never describe the numbers as citywide.

Design choices. View A uses 100% bars so allegation types of very different size can be compared as shares, with each type’s n printed next to it so a 17% from 23 allegations is not mistaken for a 14% from 166. The three findings keep the same order and colour in every segment, are named in a legend, and are labelled directly on the bars where they fit. View B shows the yearly rate with 95% Wilson intervals, hollow dots for small years, and a sample-size strip, because many years rest on very few allegations. View C answers a question the headline number invites: “substantiated” does not always mean formal charges. Only … of substantiated allegations ended in charges; the rest ended in command discipline, training or instructions. All value axes start at zero.

Transformations, stated plainly. We grouped the board’s detailed findings (for example “Substantiated (Command Discipline A)”) into three headline findings by their first word, and grouped the bracketed part into four outcome families for View C. View B uses year closed from 2002 to 2020; two earlier allegations are omitted and 2020 is partial.

What it shows. Overall, … of allegations in this subset were substantiated. The rate differs by type: … for Abuse of Authority against … for Force (… allegations). Year to year the rate bounces around a lot, and the intervals show most of that movement is within the noise. Every value is reachable by hover, keyboard focus, or the data table under each chart, and the three colours were checked for colour-blind separation.

Black hat visualization Persuasive · selective · misleading

The story we want to sell: “The NYPD is cracking down on misconduct.” Every number below is real. The picture is built to leave a false impression. Use the switches to undo each trick and watch what it was hiding.

Undo the tricks

Write-up: black hat

The manipulation. We start from a true fact: the share of allegations substantiated was … for cases closed in 2008 and … for 2015. Then we make it look like a trend and a policy result.

1. Cherry-picked window. The chart covers 2008–2015 only, starting at the lowest year and ending at the highest. In 2005 the rate was already about …. After 2015 it fell back to … in 2016 and … in 2018. Those years are simply left off.

2. Truncated y-axis. The baseline sits at 5% and is never labelled, and the bars are drawn up from it. The 2008 bar is almost invisible and the 2015 bar towers over it, so the picture shows a jump of well over 100× while the real ratio is …. Bar length is supposed to mean value; here it does not.

3. Hidden sample sizes. The 2008 rate rests on only … allegations (exactly … of them substantiated) and 2015 on …. A rate from a handful of cases is very noisy, but with no n and no interval the reader cannot tell.

4. Loaded language and framing. “Cracks down”, “upheld” and “cases” suggest enforcement and results. The data counts allegations, and “substantiated” covers everything from formal charges down to instructions or training (see White hat View C). The headline multiplies two rates and presents the ratio as a policy outcome with no causal evidence behind it.

5. Emphasis and framing. A tall, narrow frame steepens the slope, the final bar is darker, a red arrow and “×” callout direct the eye, and no source or caveat is shown. All of this stops once you tick the last switch. The same data supports a very different, much more cautious message.

Extra credit features

  • “Undo the tricks” switches (black hat). Four independent checkboxes reverse each manipulation (window, axis, hidden sample sizes, title/label/source) with animated transitions and a plain-language note on what changed. This goes beyond showing a deceptive chart: the reader can take it apart and see each distortion’s individual effect.
  • Uncertainty and sample size in the white hat view. Wilson 95% intervals, hollow markers for small samples, and an aligned sample-size strip, so the honest chart also shows how much the data can support.
  • Accessibility. Hover and keyboard-focus tooltips, a data table for every white hat chart, a colour palette checked for colour-blind separation, and reduced-motion support.
